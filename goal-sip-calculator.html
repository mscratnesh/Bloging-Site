Risk profile test & goal SIP calculator

Answer 10 short questions to find your risk profile, then add up to six goals to see the monthly SIP, step-up SIP or lumpsum each one needs. It takes about five minutes. We don’t ask for your name, phone or email, and your answers stay in your browser.

How this calculator works

What your risk profile measures

A risk profile describes how much short-term loss your money can take on its way to a goal. This test looks at it from two sides, because both matter and they often disagree.

Risk capacity is how much risk your situation can absorb: your age, how steady your income is, how many people depend on you, whether you have an emergency fund, and how soon you may need the money. Questions 1 to 5 measure it.

Risk willingness is how much risk you are comfortable with: how you would react to a sharp fall, how much investing you have done, what you want the money to do, the range of yearly outcomes you would accept, and what return you expect. Questions 6 to 10 measure it.

Each question has four answers scored 1 to 4, so each side scores between 5 and 20. Your profile is set by the lower of the two scores. A young salaried investor with no dependents may have high capacity, but if a 25% fall would lead them to sell everything, the lower willingness score decides. The reverse also holds: enthusiasm for equity doesn’t help if the money is needed in two years.

When the two scores are six or more points apart, the report says which side is holding your profile back, so you know what would change it.

From profile to asset mix

Each profile maps to a mix of three broad asset classes: equity, debt and gold. The mix is not a list of funds; it only says how much of a goal’s money would sit in each kind of asset.

The full profile mix applies only to goals seven or more years away. Money needed sooner has less time to recover from a fall, so the calculator caps equity for shorter goals: no equity or gold below three years, at most 30% equity from three to five years, and at most 50% equity from five to seven years. Whatever equity is removed moves to debt.

How the SIP for each goal is worked out

1. Today’s cost becomes a future cost

Prices rise, so a goal that costs ₹10,00,000 today will cost more when you reach it. The calculator grows today’s cost by the goal’s inflation rate for each year until the goal. At 10% a year, ₹10,00,000 becomes about ₹25,93,742 in ten years. Each goal type starts with its own default inflation rate (education costs, for example, tend to rise faster than car prices), and you can change any of them.

2. Savings you already have count first

If you have already set money aside for a goal, the calculator grows it at the goal’s blended rate and subtracts it from the future cost. What is left is the gap your SIP has to fill. If your savings already cover it, the goal shows as funded on these assumptions.

3. A blended rate for the goal’s mix

The gap is filled using an illustrative yearly rate for each asset class, weighted by the goal’s mix. These rates are assumptions you can edit, not a forecast or a promise, and actual returns will differ, sometimes by a lot. The yearly rate is converted to an equivalent monthly rate.

4. Monthly SIP, step-up SIP and lumpsum

The monthly SIP is the fixed amount, invested at the start of every month, that grows to the gap by the goal date. The step-up SIP is a lower starting amount that you raise by a set percentage every year, which suits a growing income. The lumpsum is the single amount that would grow to the gap if invested today.

Retirement

For retirement you enter your monthly expenses in today’s money and how many years you expect retirement to last. The calculator inflates the expense to your retirement date, then works out the corpus that could pay it every month, rising with inflation each year, while the remaining money earns an illustrative post-retirement rate. That corpus becomes the retirement goal.

When the SIPs add up to more than you can invest

The calculator compares the total SIP across your goals with the monthly amount you said you can invest. If it doesn’t fit, your surplus goes to high-priority goals first, then medium, then low. For any goal left short, the report shows four levers you could pull: giving the goal more time, raising the SIP every year, lowering the target, or adding a lumpsum. Each lever is worked out on its own, so you can see which matters most for that goal.

What this tool does not do

It doesn’t name or suggest any mutual fund scheme, and it doesn’t cover insurance, tax or loans. The result is an indicative risk profile and a set of SIP estimates under assumptions you can see and change. For personalised advice, review the result with a SEBI-registered Investment Adviser.

Frequently asked questions

Is my data saved or sent anywhere?

No. The calculator runs entirely in your browser. Your answers are not stored on our server and are not sent anywhere. Closing or refreshing the page clears them.

Why is my profile set by the lower of the two scores?

Because either side can cause a plan to fail. If your situation can’t absorb a loss, a fall can force you to sell at the wrong time. If you aren’t comfortable with a loss, you may sell anyway. Using the lower score keeps the mix within both limits.

Are the return rates in the calculator a forecast?

No. They are illustrative assumptions used only for the arithmetic. Mutual fund returns are not guaranteed and can be negative. You can change every rate and see how the SIPs move.

What is a step-up SIP?

A SIP whose monthly amount rises by a fixed percentage every year, for example 10%. Because later instalments are larger, the starting amount needed for the same goal is lower than a flat SIP.

Why does a goal three years away have no equity?

Below three years the calculator keeps the goal’s money entirely in debt, because there may not be enough time to recover from a fall in equity or gold before the money is needed.

Does this tool recommend which funds to buy?

No. It shows an asset-class mix and SIP amounts only. It is not investment advice. For personalised advice, speak to a SEBI-registered Investment Adviser.
